The recognizable gait I've become accustomed to hearing approaches. Patiently, I wait as the back door is opened. An initial series of steps, the fence nudged open, and then a few more. I thud onto the cement block before the grass. Walking carefully around the sweetgum balls barely perceptible as the sun comes out, I make my rounds.

Later, I hear the clink of a metal collar and the atmosphere in the house changes. A shared giddiness and half-hearted attempts at maintaining composure as the collar is clasped on.

The garage door scrolls open or the front door swings and a second rush of adrenaline overwhelms me. Even as the chiding grows louder it grows further as my attention is brought to my surroundings.

The street is crossed to safety, the ditch leaped over. The grass is more malleable than it is at home. My attention flares as the fenced dogs a distance away bark in my direction. I hold back retorts to avoid being sent back home. A clearing is reached. Briefly vigilant, I walk in slow circles before settling down and closing my eyes.

The Lisbon Earthquake of 17551 struck on the morning of November 1st, All Saints' Day. It was one of the most destructive earthquakes in European history. The earthquake was followed by fires and a tsunami, which together devastated the city of Lisbon, Portugal. Estimates of the death toll vary, but tens of thousands of people perished. The city itself was largely reduced to rubble.

The timing of the disaster carried particular weight. All Saints' Day was one of the most significant dates in the Catholic calendar. Churches were full of worshippers, candles were burning throughout the city, and the population was engaged in religious observance. The earthquake struck in the morning, collapsing churches and killing those gathered inside them. The fires that followed burned for days.

The disaster provoked an immediate philosophical and theological crisis across Europe. The central question was how a benevolent, all-powerful God could permit such an event. This question had existed in philosophy and theology before 1755, but the scale and visibility of the Lisbon disaster brought it into urgent public debate. It became one of the defining intellectual crises of the Enlightenment.

The town square razed with destruction. Trembling palpitations of the heart. The mind remains focused on nothing and everything. Attempts at calming myself through my breadth. A single event can drive sentiment in a negative direction during a time when positive thoughts had been affluent.

Candide2 is the central character of Voltaire's 1759 novella of the same name. He is a young man of uncertain noble birth raised in the comfort of a Westphalian baron's castle, tutored by the philosopher Pangloss in the belief that the world is fundamentally good and everything happens for the best.

When he is expelled, that optimism collides with reality — war, natural disaster, enslavement. The tension between what Candide has been taught and what he witnesses drives the entire story. He travels across Europe and the Americas, motivated largely by his love for Cunégonde, encountering suffering at every turn and characters who each offer their own philosophy in response to it.

By the end, Candide abandons grand theorizing entirely. He and his companions settle on a small farm, and his famous conclusion — that one must cultivate one's garden — signals a turn away from abstract reasoning toward grounded, practical work. The garden is not a retreat from the world but an engagement with it: tending what is immediately before you, producing something real, and finding meaning in that labor. Voltaire's ending suggests that the most honest response to a chaotic world is not a better philosophy, but a functioning garden.

Cultivating a garden. Labor for labor's sake emits a quiet despondence. Natural in Candide's circumstances given what he has been through. Not evil or idealistic, everyday life enables realism but the soul hopes to elicit wonder.

In his 1784 essay What is Enlightenment, Kant defines enlightenment as the emergence from self-incurred immaturity3. Immaturity, on his account, is the inability to use one's own understanding without the guidance of another. He calls this self-incurred because its cause is not a lack of understanding but a lack of courage to use it without direction from an outside authority. His remedy is captured in a phrase borrowed from the Roman poet Horace: Sapere aude, dare to know. Kant argues that all that is required for enlightenment is freedom, specifically the freedom to make public use of one's reason in all matters.

Immanuel Kant held that the fundamental principle of morality is what he called the categorical imperative, a command that applies unconditionally to all rational beings regardless of their desires, goals, or circumstances. Unlike hypothetical imperatives, which bind a person only on the condition that they pursue some particular goal, the categorical imperative binds all rational people simply by virtue of their capacity for reason. Kant formulated this principle in several ways, the most basic of which instructs a person to act only on principles that they could consistently will to become universal laws applying to everyone equally. To assess whether a maxim meets this standard, Kant proposed a thought experiment in which one asks whether the principle underlying an action could be universalized without contradiction or incoherence.

His own illustration involved the keeping of deposits — if someone adopted the maxim that it is permissible to deny having received a deposit when no proof exists, and this maxim were universalized, the very institution of deposits would collapse entirely, since no one would entrust anything to another person under such conditions. The maxim therefore fails the test because it destroys the practice it depends upon the moment it is made universal. A second formulation of the same principle instructs that one must treat humanity, whether in oneself or in any other person, always as an end and never merely as a means, on the grounds that rational beings possess a dignity that does not derive from their usefulness to others but from their capacity for rational self-governance. Kant regarded these formulations as equivalent expressions of the same underlying moral law, which he held to be a product of reason itself rather than of custom, feeling, or external authority.
