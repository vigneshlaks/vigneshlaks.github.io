Bruno

The recognizable gait I've become accustomed to hearing approaches. Patiently, I wait as the back door is opened. An initial series of steps, the fence nudged open, and then a few more. I thud onto the cement block before the grass. Walking carefully around the sweetgum balls barely perceptible as the sun comes out, I make my rounds.

Later in the day, I hear the clink of a metal collar and the atmosphere in the house changes. A shared giddiness and half-hearted attempts at maintaining composure as the collar is clasped on. The garage door scrolls open or the front door swings and a second rush of adrenaline overwhelms me. Even as the chiding grows louder it grows further as my attention is brought to my surroundings.

The carpeted texture of home instead asphalt, concrete, and gravel. The scent of turmeric and masala powder now a neutral one. A siren approaches and the ground tilts. Maybe another dog or even a wolf but more likely an ambulance. I echo the tune before continuing.

The street is crossed to safety, the ditch leaped over. The grass is more malleable than it is at home. My attention flares as the fenced dogs a distance away bark in my direction. I hold back retorts to avoid being sent back home.

A familiar spirit appears before me. We reach a clearing as she lies flat onto the crown of my head and her breathing begins to slow. Briefly vigilant, I walk in slow circles before settling down and closing my eyes.

I remember this place from an earlier visit and can hear a stream. The Tagus. Stretching from near the middle of Spain through Portugal to the Atlantic Ocean. The spirit appears and conjures a note. A process I have become familiar with.

The Lisbon Earthquake of 17551 struck on the morning of November 1st, All Saints' Day. It was one of the most destructive earthquakes in European history. The earthquake was followed by fires and a tsunami, which together devastated the city of Lisbon, Portugal. Estimates of the death toll vary, but tens of thousands of people perished. The city itself was largely reduced to rubble.

The timing of the disaster carried particular weight. All Saints' Day was one of the most significant dates in the Catholic calendar. Churches were full of worshippers, candles were burning throughout the city, and the population was engaged in religious observance. The earthquake struck in the morning, collapsing churches and killing those gathered inside them. The fires that followed burned for days.

The disaster provoked an immediate philosophical and theological crisis across Europe. The central question was how a benevolent, all-powerful God could permit such an event. This question had existed in philosophy and theology before 1755, but the scale and visibility of the Lisbon disaster brought it into urgent public debate. It became one of the defining intellectual crises of the Enlightenment.

I have seen this one before. The town square razed with destruction. Attempts at calming myself through my breadth. Alternating my attention between the initial moment of redirection and as it hits the top of my diaphragm. A single event can drive sentiment in a negative direction during a time when positive thoughts had been affluent.

Candide2 is the central character of Voltaire's 1759 novella of the same name. He is a young man of uncertain noble birth who grows up in the castle of a Westphalian baron, where he is tutored by the philosopher Pangloss. His early life is sheltered and comfortable, and he absorbs Pangloss's teachings without question.

The philosophy Candide is raised on holds that the world is fundamentally good and that everything happens for the best. When he is expelled from the castle, he carries this view with him into a series of violent and chaotic experiences, including war, natural disaster, and enslavement. His responses to these events are shaped by the tension between what he has been taught to believe and what he directly observes.

Throughout the story, Candide travels across Europe and the Americas, encountering a wide range of characters and situations. He is motivated largely by his desire to reunite with Cunégonde, a woman he loves. His journey brings him into contact with suffering on a large scale, and the characters around him offer various philosophical responses to that suffering.

By the end of the novella, Candide arrives at a quieter and more practical outlook. He and his companions settle on a small farm, and he concludes that the most meaningful response to troubles is to focus on simple, productive work. This ending is often read as Voltaire's own commentary on the limits of abstract philosophical reasoning.

The conclusion appears a bit quaint. There is nothing wrong with an optimistic outlook. Candide became discouraged towards a simpler life but ultimately hardship is what builds us. I trod down a block to see the town in a state of rebuilding3. Partial wooden structures line the streets with uniform four story buildings and right-angled junctions disappearing into the horizon.

Light enters through the panes of glass illuminating my surroundings. I am in someone's house and enclosed in a cell. Naps have been frequent recently. I inhale. The thrum of the exhale causes a slight vibration on my vocal cords. After a few repetitions, I eventually fall into a slumber. Upon waking, a note materializes before me.

In his 1784 essay What is Enlightenment, Kant defines enlightenment as the emergence from self-incurred immaturity4. Immaturity, on his account, is the inability to use one's own understanding without the guidance of another. He calls this self-incurred because its cause is not a lack of understanding but a lack of courage to use it without direction from an outside authority. His remedy is captured in a phrase borrowed from the Roman poet Horace: Sapere aude, dare to know. Kant argues that all that is required for enlightenment is freedom, specifically the freedom to make public use of one's reason in all matters.

Developing the confidence and willingness to act on our own thoughts is important. As I have matured, I have stopped myself from knocking the kibble from its bowl, from lying on the couch for it offends my mother, or muddying the carpeted floors for it again offends my mother. Yet there has been support. My parents who had raised me. My siblings who had trained me.

Immanuel Kant held that the fundamental principle of morality is what he called the categorical imperative, a command that applies unconditionally to all rational beings regardless of their desires, goals, or circumstances. Unlike hypothetical imperatives, which bind a person only on the condition that they pursue some particular goal, the categorical imperative binds all rational people simply by virtue of their capacity for reason. Kant formulated this principle in several ways, the most basic of which instructs a person to act only on principles that they could consistently will to become universal laws applying to everyone equally. To assess whether a maxim meets this standard, Kant proposed a thought experiment in which one asks whether the principle underlying an action could be universalized without contradiction or incoherence. His own illustration involved the keeping of deposits — if someone adopted the maxim that it is permissible to deny having received a deposit when no proof exists, and this maxim were universalized, the very institution of deposits would collapse entirely, since no one would entrust anything to another person under such conditions. The maxim therefore fails the test because it destroys the practice it depends upon the moment it is made universal. A second formulation of the same principle instructs that one must treat humanity, whether in oneself or in any other person, always as an end and never merely as a means, on the grounds that rational beings possess a dignity that does not derive from their usefulness to others but from their capacity for rational self-governance. Kant regarded these formulations as equivalent expressions of the same underlying moral law, which he held to be a product of reason itself rather than of custom, feeling, or external authority.

Trust is built over a long period yet can quickly break down. Actions capable of being perceived as violations of trust regardless of pure underlying intentions sows discomfort. Discomfort can build to distrust. Each violation makes the neighbor's more tempting to protect themselves and their people. The cycle continues and human cooperation deteriorates.

I find myself in a library. Endless rows of books. I walk the aisles. An odd comfort overwhelms me. The accumulated thoughts of those who have come before me and meddled with ideas. Juxtaposing the earlier transportations as I finally find myself at peace.

Hegel's5 image of the owl of Minerva captures a specific claim about when philosophical understanding becomes possible. The owl is the symbol of wisdom, and Minerva is its goddess. The owl does not fly at dawn, when the day is beginning, or at midday, when it is fully underway. It flies at dusk, when the light is already fading and the day is drawing to its close. Hegel uses this image to say that a form of life, a set of social arrangements, cultural values, historical conditions, can only be philosophically comprehended once it has already reached its completion and begun to dissolve. Philosophy arrives at the end, not the beginning. While a particular way of organizing social and cultural life is still operative, its foundational assumptions function as the invisible medium through which everything is seen rather than as one of the things being examined. They are too immediate, too close, too thoroughly woven into the fabric of daily existence to be available for philosophical scrutiny.

It is only once a form of life has worked through its internal contradictions and begun to give way to something new that the shape of the whole becomes available for comprehension. The process of dissolution is also the process of clarification. As the structures of a particular historical world loosen their grip on the people living within them, as the assumptions that once seemed self-evident begin to be questioned and the institutions that once seemed permanent begin to show their cracks, the philosopher gains access to what was always there but never fully visible — the internal logic that animated the form of life, the principle it was organized around, and the contradictions that it could not finally resolve.

If philosophy is defined here as something done in retrospect then what could be its purpose? We have power over the future as a consequence of the present. The mind loves to aimlessly wander. The past is one of its favorite places to go. A decision is made based on the judgements of our lived experiences. The present transitions to the past. The reward or consequence of our actions lay before us. The mind heals, forms calluses, and ultimately begins to form thoughts and decisions anew.

Sittlichkeit is the term Hegel uses for the ethical substance of social life, derived from the German word for custom. It names the layer of shared practices, conventions, and arrangements that a community lives by before any of its members have reflected on them or chosen them. Individuals are born into communities that already have established ways of organizing family life, civic obligation, religious practice, and economic exchange, and they absorb these patterns through the process of growing up, long before they have the conceptual resources to examine or question what they have absorbed. The ethical substance of a community is on this account not primarily the product of individual rational deliberation but something that exists prior to and independently of any particular person's reflective choices.

Hegel identifies three nested structures through which this ethical life takes shape. The family is the most immediate, held together by sentiment and love rather than calculation or contract, in which the individual tends to be absorbed into the social unit as a part of a whole rather than standing apart as a separate self. Civil society represents the contrasting principle, the world of economic exchange in which individuals encounter each other as distinct self-identical subjects pursuing their own ends through external relationships. These two principles pull against each other, each one-sided in its own way, and the state is described as the overarching institutional framework whose role is to hold both within a single structure, each mediating and tempering the other.

By the end of his account Hegel has arrived at a position that refuses the picture of the individual as a bare rational agent who exists prior to social life and then steps into it by choice. The wants, the reasoning capacities, the moral sensibilities, and the sense of what matters that any individual brings to their deliberations were already shaped by the community that formed them before any deliberation was possible. No one stands entirely outside the social relationships and customs that made them who they are. Reflection on those customs is possible, but it happens from within a social world the individual did not create and cannot fully step outside of.

Just as the opinion of other's is shaped through an aggregation of our actions so too do our own thoughts become shaped by those around us. Antagonistic at its worst, interactions can move towards being positive sum with deliberate intention.

—

The steps back home are retraced and the same sights I have become familiar with appear once more. I remain enthusiastic as ever. Eventually, the front door opens then the screen door. I settle onto the tiled floor cool against my skin. Breathing down past my chest and into my diaphragm as I settle myself for what the rest of the day may have to offer.
